01 · Key insights

Difficulty concentrates where the practice designs it to — Stage 3 above all — and Positive Affect still leads throughout

Every figure below is a pooled mean or count across all 614 responses — no individual row is shown or shown-through anywhere in this report. Stage design intent (which stages are meant to be hard) is drawn from twelve-stages-design-map, a facilitator-level reference obtained after this report's first draft.

measured

Anxiety and Agitation both peak sharply at Stage 3 — the one stage whose own design names cortisol

Anxiety hits 3.09/5 at Stage 3 (Threshold) against a pooled mean of 1.90 elsewhere; Agitation hits 2.81/5 against a pooled 1.91. This isn't a diffuse "minority of responses" pattern — it's concentrated exactly at the stage the practice's own facilitator design names as its cortisol/exhaustion point ("Have you had enough? Are you exhausted?"). Fear peaks at Stage 4 (3.25/5, n=4 — small sample) alongside Confusion (2.75/5).

Positive Affect still beats Negative Affect at all 12 stages, including Stage 3

On the PANAS-X-derived 14-item battery, Positive Affect stays above Negative Affect from Stage 1 (Safety) through Stage 12 (Re-integration) — including Stage 3–4, now read as the design's intended difficulty peak rather than an unexplained "crisis theme." The 60-item PANAS-X checklist agrees on the same shape: difficulty registers, but doesn't dominate, even where it's meant to show up.

Serenity and self-assurance dominate the top emotions

Calm, relaxed, at ease, inspired, strong, confident — no negative-affect item cracks the top 20 pooled emotions out of 60. The somatic top-line (energy waves, floating, visions, chest expansion) reads as engagement, not distress, at the aggregate level.

measured

Engagement is heavily front-loaded

Stages 1–4 account for 74% of all 614 responses. Stages 10–12 — arguably the most consequential for a "does the arc complete" claim — have only 6, 3, and 5 participants respectively. Any per-stage claim past Stage 9 is provisional.

This is a reviewer sample, not a naive-user sample

H3
Name the minority distress-adjacent phenomena in informed consent, cheaply
Prioritize now

Confusion, agitation, anxiety, fear, and sudden loss of awareness are each reported by 13–21% of responses (low mean intensity). For a generally healthy but stress-exposed population — who may not be screened the way an existing practitioner community is — a short informed-consent note and facilitator awareness of these specific items is a near-zero-cost, safety-positive step, and echoes the Living Neuroscience module's own caution about vulnerable nervous systems.

H8
Test whether a naive, non-practitioner sample reports the same profile
Design a pilot

This dataset is reviewers auditioning tracks against an intended stage — very likely existing practitioners. The whole positive-skew finding (H2 below) rests on generalizing from this sample to "a generally healthy, stress-exposed population," which is exactly the population this sample is not guaranteed to represent. A small pilot with unaffiliated, screened-for-no-prior-exposure participants would test the single biggest validity assumption underneath every other hypothesis here.

H5
Collect more Stage 9–12 responses before relying on the "full recovery" arc
Quick win

Concept Overview's resilience-arc / toughening-model hypothesis (Stage 4 stress → Stage 10 full recovery) is built on the HRV pilot, not this survey. Here, Negative Affect does not decline monotonically through Stage 9 (NA≈2.9), and Stage 10–11 are too thin (n=6, n=3) to confirm or contradict a clean recovery shape. This is a data-collection gap, not a finding — worth closing before the toughening-model story is used in any external-facing claim.

Quick win

H6
Give Stage 10 ("Dark Night") extra facilitator attention ahead of wider rollout
Proceed carefully

Stage 10 shows the highest Interoception/Cognition/Movement domain intensity of any stage in the physical checklist, at the same time as its own name signals it's meant to be the hardest stage. With only 6 participants, this could easily be noise — but if a stage is both self-reported as high-intensity and is the practice's own crisis stage, it's a reasonable place to prioritize facilitator support ahead of scaling to a less-experienced population, before waiting for a larger sample to confirm.

H2
Position innerdance as a controlled-difficulty coactivation practice, not a purely calming one
Design a pilot

Revised from an earlier draft, which read the positive-affect-dominant profile as support for a "serenity practice" positioning. That was a misreading: twelve-stages-design-map shows Stage 3–4 are designed activation/difficulty points (cortisol named explicitly at Stage 3), not incidental crisis theming, and the Research Program's own Phase II hypothesis is a coactivation/toughening model — controlled stress followed by full recovery — not a relaxation model. The positioning this data actually supports, pending the naive-sample check (H8): a practice that deliberately includes a brief, contained difficulty peak (Stage 3–4) as part of how it produces its recovery/wellbeing effect, not one that avoids difficulty altogether.

H4
Pair this self-report instrument with the HRV pipeline to test a vagal mechanism
Design a pilot

Nelson's vlPAG/vagus mechanism (spiritual-doorway-nelson) predicts that parasympathetic/vagal engagement — not just relaxation in general — should track with the Serenity-family affect reported here. This survey's top physical items (energy waves, chest expansion, floating) are at least plausibly vagally-mediated, and free-text mentions of yawning point the same direction. Running the existing HRV biofeedback pipeline (SD2/RMSSD) alongside this PANAS-X instrument, on the same sessions, is the most direct test — but it's a real study design, not a re-analysis, and the biggest lift of the eight.

06 · Methodology & limitations

What this report is built on, and where it's assuming

On "the Research Program's objective": at this report's original writing, no file named "Research Program" existed in the repo, and Concept Overview's generic phase table was used as a stand-in proxy. The actual document was added afterward as research-program and Hypotheses above are now framed against its real Phase II coactivation/toughening-model hypothesis, not the proxy.
On stage design intent: this report's original Key Insights (Section 1) and Hypothesis 2 read the low Negative-Affect pattern as uniformly reassuring, including at the "crisis-themed" stages. twelve-stages-design-map — a facilitator-level design reference obtained after the first draft — showed this was a misreading: Stage 3 and Stage 9 are explicitly designed to surface fear, exhaustion, and failure. Sections 1–3 and Hypothesis 2 have been corrected accordingly; this callout records that the correction happened and why.
De-identification: the raw survey file (614 rows, including email addresses and free text) never enters this report or the tracked knowledge base. It lives in a gitignored local folder. Every number above is a pooled mean, count, or percentage computed across the full sample; the handful of free-text illustrations are generic sensory descriptions, translated, hand-reviewed for identifying content (none found), and can't be traced back to a respondent.
  • Self-selection. Respondents are reviewing/auditioning tracks, not undergoing a naive first exposure — see Hypothesis 8.
  • Stage imbalance. Stages 10–12 have 3–6 participants each; treat any claim about them as provisional.
  • No physiological measurement. Everything here is 1–5 self-report; it cannot be cross-checked against HRV, EEG, or any objective marker in this vault.
  • Translation equivalence untested. 58% of responses are non-English; whether PANAS-X's validated properties survive translation here wasn't established.
  • This report mixes two epistemic modes. Sections 1–4 are vault-grounded aggregate statistics. Section 5's hypotheses are original inference built on top of that data plus general research-design reasoning — not themselves vault-cited facts.
