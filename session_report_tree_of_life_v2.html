📖 Understanding the HRV Metrics

Heart Rate Variability (HRV) measures the fluctuation in time between consecutive heartbeats (R-R intervals). A healthy autonomic nervous system produces irregular intervals — this variability is adaptive, not noise. The reported metrics were chosen because they give an overall picture of the variability. Clinical norms throughout this table are from one consolidated source, Shaffer & Ginsberg (2017), Front. Public Health 5:258.

← swipe to see the full table →

Metric What it measures Why chosen for this study Clinical evidence Subjective experience Validity caveat
Heart Rate
Mean HR · bpm		 Overall autonomic arousal/activation level — the simplest, most direct signal: faster heart = more sympathetic drive, slower = more parasympathetic. Always valid, at any breathing rate — the one metric here with no window-length or breathing-rate caveat, so it anchors the others. Standard vital sign; resting HR strongly predicts cardiovascular outcomes (Shaffer & Ginsberg (2017), Front. Public Health 5:258). When lower: settled, grounded. When higher: activated, alert, possibly effortful. ✓ Always valid — no caveat.
RMSSD
Root Mean Square of
Successive Differences · ms		 Beat-to-beat parasympathetic (vagal) tone. Captures how much each heartbeat deviates from the one before it. Gold-standard resting vagal marker; simple to compute; provides the pre-session baseline anchor and the personal RMSSD reference. Requires a 5-min resting window. Higher resting RMSSD associated with lower all-cause mortality, reduced depression and anxiety risk, better cardiovascular resilience. Age-stratified population norms: median age 40–50 ≈ 38 ms; athletes typically 60–100 ms; sedentary adults 20–40 ms (Shaffer & Ginsberg (2017), Front. Public Health 5:258). When high: chest opens, breathing deepens, a felt sense of ease and safety. When low: a subtle tightening, more rigid rhythm — the body is mobilising, not distressed. ⚠ Invalid during innerdance when breathing <9 bpm — RSA moves out of the HF band, corrupting RMSSD (McCreary et al. 2022, Am J Physiol 323:R519–R531, doi:10.1152/ajpregu.00124.2022). Use only against resting baseline.
SD2
Poincaré long-axis · ms		 Long-term/total R-R variability — the long axis of the Poincaré (beat-vs-next-beat) plot. Captures both sympathetic and parasympathetic contributions together; the "operating range" of the ANS. Unlike RMSSD, SD2 remains valid during slow breathing, so it is the within-session flexibility marker used alongside DFA α1 in the rolling trend view. Reduced SD2 associated with higher cardiovascular risk, same direction as reduced RMSSD/SDNN (Shaffer & Ginsberg (2017), Front. Public Health 5:258). When high: a sense of spaciousness, able to be both alert and calm within the same session. When low: a narrower, more constrained range; "locked in" to a state, less able to shift — not necessarily bad if it means deep, sustained focus. ⚠ Needs a 5-min-or-longer window to be reliable — reported pre/post, plus a 5-min rolling view (interpret with caution); not a per-stage value.
SD1/SD2
Poincaré ratio · unitless		 Ratio of the Poincaré plot's short axis (SD1, fast beat-to-beat/vagal) to its long axis (SD2, total variability) — the balance of variability, not the amount of it. Complements SD2: two sessions with the same SD2 can still differ in how much of that variability is fast/vagal vs. slower — this ratio is what tells them apart. Lower ratio (vagal share of total variability shrinking) tracks the same direction as reduced RMSSD/SDNN in cardiovascular-risk studies (Shaffer & Ginsberg (2017), Front. Public Health 5:258). Closer to 1: balanced, open, variability evenly spread between fast and slow. Closer to 0: narrower, more sympathetic-leaning range. ⚠ Same 5-min-window caveat as SD2 — not a per-stage value.
DFA α1
Detrended Fluctuation
Analysis · exponent		 Short-range fractal scaling of the R-R series (scales 4–16 beats). Measures whether the heartbeat has self-similar temporal structure or is losing that organisation (<0.75). The only nonlinear-complexity metric in the set. Uniquely sensitive to loss of autonomic regulation at SNS peaks. Less affected by slow breathing than RMSSD. DFA α1 <0.75 associated with cardiac arrhythmia, overtraining syndrome, and acute autonomic dysregulation; rigid/elderly >1.5 (Shaffer & Ginsberg (2017), Front. Public Health 5:258; original method: Peng et al. 1995, Chaos 5(1):82–87, doi:10.1063/1.166141). Around 1.0: flow, timelessness — the heartbeat organises itself effortlessly. Below 0.75: maximum energy, minimum order — like the moment before a wave breaks. Above 1.3: deep rest or rigidity — the system is no longer adapting, just sustaining. ⚠ Slow breathing artificially lowers α1 — ambiguous without respiratory rate (McCreary 2022).

🎵 Why These Acoustic Features?

Every acoustic feature this pipeline extracts, unranked — each maps onto a distinct dimension of auditory perception known to drive autonomic nervous system responses.

← swipe to see the full table →

Feature Why it matters physiologically What an innerdance expert hears / feels Key literature
Tempo Instability
CV of beat-period estimates across overlapping 4 s windows 		Sudden tempo shifts disrupt hippocampal time-cell prediction loops, triggering subcortical/reticular/vestibular activation. The nervous system can't predict 'when next' — this generates arousal independently of volume. Does the rhythm feel unpredictable or unstable? High instability = the track keeps surprising the body's motor system; low = steady hypnotic pulse. Expert feedback June 2026. Beat induction / basal ganglia: Grahn & Rowe (2009) J Neurosci 29(23):7540–8. Neural entrainment: Nozaradan et al. (2011) J Neurosci 31(27):10234–40.
Rhythm Clarity
CV of the onset strength envelope (std/mean) 		Metrically clear rhythm (high CV: strong beats punctuate silence) vs. ambient / drone texture (low CV: steady undifferentiated energy). Clear rhythm enables neural beat-locking (motor–auditory coupling) which modulates HR and can sustain SNS activation. Does the track have a clear, identifiable beat (High) or is it a continuous texture where you cannot tap along (Low)? S3 and S11+12 score High via different mechanisms. Grahn & Brett (2007) Neuropsychologia; Nozaradan et al. (2011) J Neurosci
Sub-Bass Vibration
RMS amplitude in 20–250 Hz band 		Sub-bass and low-frequency vibration activates the vestibular system and somatic (tactile) receptors in the chest and abdomen. Strong bass is associated with increased HR and SNS activation, and also with trance-induction via repetitive low-frequency entrainment. Felt as physical vibration in the sternum, belly, and legs. Experienced practitioners describe strong bass as 'grounding' or 'destabilising' depending on context. Peak at Stage 7–8. Juslin & Västfjäll (2008) Behav Brain Sci 31(5):559–621; Todd & Cody (2000) J Acoust Soc Am 108(6):2974–82. Vestibular saccular activation: Todd & Cody 2000. Emotion via music: Juslin & Västfjäll 2008.
Tonal Brightness
Hz frequency of the spectral centre of mass 		High-frequency content is perceived as 'sharp' or 'harsh' and triggers alerting responses via the auditory brainstem reflex. Correlated with increased skin conductance and cortisol. Low brightness (warm, muffled) is associated with parasympathetic relaxation. The tonal 'colour' of the track — dark and warm (low) vs. bright and piercing (high). S8+9 (highest centroid ≈1431 Hz) sounds metallic and intense; S11+12 (≈378 Hz) sounds warm and rounded. Leman et al. (2005) Musicae Scientiae; Eerola & Vuoskoski (2011) Music Percept
Vocal / Harmonic Content
RMS amplitude in 250 Hz – 2 kHz band 		The midrange band covers human voice and most melodic content. Vocal presence activates mirror-neuron and social-affective processing networks, modulating vagal tone. High mid-energy stages tend to feel interpersonally connected. Can you hear a voice, a melody, or a harmonic instrument? High = present and melodic; Low = bass-heavy drone or ambient wash with no tonal identity. Juslin & Västfjäll (2008) Behav Brain Sci; Trost et al. (2012) Neuropsychologia
Tonal vs. Noise Quality
Ratio of geometric to arithmetic mean of power spectrum 		Near 0 = strongly tonal (pure tone-like). Near 1 = noise-like / white noise. Tonal signals trigger pitch-processing and melodic expectation; noise-like signals activate diffuse arousal systems without melodic expectation. Stage 1 is almost a pure tone (flatness ≈0) — resonant bowl or sine-wave drone. Stages with high flatness feel 'textured', 'rough', or 'oceanic' — no single pitch to latch onto. McAdams & Bregman (1979) J Acoust Soc Am; Leman et al. (2005) Musicae Scientiae
Sonic Movement / Change
L2 norm of column-to-column STFT difference 		Rate of spectral change over time. High flux = timbral evolution, novel sonic events, transitions. Spectral novelty drives attention and may produce orienting responses (HR deceleration, then return). Does the sound feel like it is constantly changing, evolving (high flux) or sustaining one texture (low flux)? High at S3 and S5; low at S1 (pure drone). Trost et al. (2012) Neuropsychologia; Huron (2006) Sweet Anticipation
Bass Vibration
RMS amplitude in 20–250 Hz band (broader low-frequency band) 		Broader low-frequency energy — includes sub-bass and upper bass. Complementary to sub_bass_energy; less specific vestibular mechanism but captures overall somatic presence of low-end content. How 'heavy' or 'weighty' does the track feel? Felt in chest and floor. Overlaps with sub-bass but also picks up kick drums and bass instruments. Juslin & Västfjäll (2008) Behav Brain Sci
Brightness / Presence
RMS amplitude in 2–8 kHz band 		High-frequency band energy. Bright, present, cutting sound. Correlated with alertness and SNS arousal. H8 hypothesis: brightness above ~1100 Hz independently predicts sympathetic activation. The 'air' and 'presence' of the track — does it cut through and feel immediate? High = sharp, forward, attention-grabbing. Low = recessed, warm. Leman et al. (2005) Musicae Scientiae
Amplitude Modulation Depth
CV of Hilbert envelope smoothed at 2 Hz 		Slow amplitude fluctuations (0.5–4 Hz) overlap with the respiratory band and can entrain breathing via auditory-respiratory coupling. High AM depth creates a pulsing texture that may pace breathing and increase HRV coherence. The 'breathing' or 'swelling' quality of the track — does the sound pulse and throb (high AM) or remain steady (low AM)? Very high at S11+12. Trost et al. (2012) Neuropsychologia; Nozaradan et al. (2011) J Neurosci
Breathing Pacemaker
AM envelope power at 0.15–0.40 Hz (RSA / respiratory band) 		AM modulation in the respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) band (0.15–0.40 Hz = 9–24 bpm). When music oscillates at this rate it may entrain breathing, increasing SD2 via auditory-respiratory coupling. Highest at S12 (0.472) — confirms the closing stage paces breathing at ~10 bpm by design. A slow, regular 'breathing in and out' quality — the track rises and falls like a long breath. Very different from the fast beat of S3 or the slow tide of S9. High at S12; lower at high-energy stages. H6 hypothesis. McCreary et al. (2022) Am J Physiol 323(4):R519–R531, doi:10.1152/ajpregu.00124.2022; Lehrer & Gevirtz (2014) Front. Psychol. 5:756, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756.
Slow Pacemaker (DFA risk zone)
AM envelope power at 0.04–0.15 Hz (Welch PSD) 		Very slow AM modulation (0.04–0.15 Hz = 2.4–9 bpm). At S4 and S9 this band is highest — a risk factor: if this paces breathing below 9 bpm, DFA α1 and RMSSD become unreliable. Very slow undulation — a pulse once every 7–25 seconds. Barely perceptible as rhythm; feels more like a 'tide' or 'breath of the room'. McCreary et al. (2022) Am J Physiol 323(4):R519–R531, doi:10.1152/ajpregu.00124.2022. Slow AM at these stages may pace breathing below 9 bpm — the threshold where DFA α1 and RMSSD become unreliable.
Roughness / Noisiness
Rate of sign changes in the waveform per second 		Proxy for noisiness / percussiveness vs. tonality. High ZCR = noisy, grainy, or very high frequency. Low ZCR = smooth, low-pitched, tonal. How 'rough', 'gritty', or 'textured' does the sound feel? High = crackling fire, rain, noise; Low = organ, singing bowl, sustained pad. McAdams & Bregman (1979) J Acoust Soc Am
Beat / Pulse Presence
Mean onset strength envelope 		Captures the intensity of sound onsets (percussive events, note attacks). High onset strength indicates a driving, pulse-like character that activates the motor system via basal ganglia beat induction. How 'driven' or 'percussive' is the track? A track with sharp transients invites movement and activates; a sustained drone dissolves boundaries. Grahn & Rowe (2009) J Neurosci; Zatorre et al. (2007) Nat Rev Neurosci
